# Keep ownership of the answer

About 10 minutes to read. Optional exercise about 10 minutes. These are estimates.

Addy Osmani wrote about critical thinking in a world where agents do more of the work. He is not arguing for fewer assistants. He is arguing that you have to keep the habit of thinking, because the tools will not force it on you.

Critical thinking, as he restates it, still means meeting new information with curiosity and skepticism, taking in more than one view, and forming your own opinion. What has changed is the space between question and answer. That space used to be long enough that you had to wrestle. Now it can be seconds.

As models improve, obvious errors get rarer. Many outputs look well written and mostly correct, so they do not demand a second look. You still have only one pair of eyes, even if several assistants are running at once. The pleasantness is part of the trap. Fast, helpful answers feel like relief. Relief makes it easy to relax.

## Three places on the spectrum

Addy describes a slide that is easy to miss because each step seems reasonable.

**Offloading** is delegating a task while you still own the result. You asked for help. You can still explain the answer and stand behind it. He does this all day, and he thinks you should too.

**Deferral** is when the tool’s output becomes your new starting point, and it has stopped being a hypothesis. You are no longer asking “is this true?” You are decorating something you have already accepted.

**Surrender** is when the output quietly becomes your own, and you feel no need to check it. Addy notes that “cognitive surrender” is a term coined by Steven Shaw and Gideon Nave at Wharton. The slide looks like this: you read the last answer carefully, so you skim the next one, then you only scan the skeleton of the one after that. Later someone asks about a line and you cannot explain it, even with the assistant’s help.

That is how a kind of debt builds. You notice it when you try to use a mental model of the work and the model is not there. He points at similar ideas in software, where a codebase can look clean and still be something nobody understands well enough to change. The same thing happens when you sign off on a strategy page after reading only the headings.

You do not need to hold an entire company in your head. He notes that some teams rebuild understanding on demand, asking an assistant to explain the slice they need. That works if you know what you do not have. It fails if you think the heading was the understanding.

## Cheap mistakes and costly ones

Where the stakes are low, Addy is happy to delegate entirely. He gives a personal example: choosing one of three similar hotels for a recording, when he did not have time to read every review. He would do that again.

He would not do it when deciding how to invest money, how to advise his children, or, at work, what to build, which users a change would affect, or whether something is safe to ship.

The rule you can keep: take the handoff where being wrong is cheap. Keep the reasoning where other people will have to live with the result. Customer promises, prices, legal claims, and anything that spends money sit on the costly side, which is why the previous lesson keeps them on “ask me first.”

## Questions that pull you back up the spectrum

When he does not know what he thinks, he goes back to basic questions. Among them: How do I know I am working on the right problem? How do I know I am solving it the right way? Is there evidence that would change my mind?

Those questions work on a homepage draft as well as they work on code. If an assistant wrote “customers save 12 hours a week” and you cannot say where the 12 came from, you are not done. You are in deferral, sliding toward surrender.

Marking invented figures, as Hunt’s charter requires, is one practical way to stay at offloading.

## A concrete example

An assistant drafts a comparison page that says your repair shop is “the most trusted in the city.” That sentence is pleasant and mostly the shape of marketing. If you cannot name who trusts you, or you do not have reviews that support “most,” publishing it would be surrender. Changing it to “same-week chair repair, with photos of the last five jobs” is offloading: the assistant helped you draft, and you owned the claim.

## Hypothetical Maal example

This example is invented for teaching.

Letting an assistant sort public recipe ideas into themes is a cheap handoff. Letting it invent a customer quote about ADHD, or a retention percentage, is not. The first you can correct later without hurting anyone. The second can become a promise you never meant to make.

## What to do

**Under 2 minutes.** Write one decision you would let an assistant make alone, and one you would never hand over.

**Optional, about 10 minutes.** Take a real or pretend assistant draft. Answer the three questions, and mark every sentence you cannot explain.

## You're done when

You can name the three places on the spectrum, and you have drawn a line between cheap mistakes and costly ones.
