# A homepage that makes sense

About 11 minutes to read. Optional exercise about 15 minutes. These are estimates.

Emily Kramer opens her homepage guide with a feeling most of us know. She looks at a startup site and cannot tell what the company does. If she can guess the product, she still cannot tell who it is for or why anyone would choose it.

When that happens, every visit you worked to get is leaking. The homepage is not a brand mood board. It is the first serious explanation a stranger will read.

Her essay focuses on copy and content, which she calls the fuel of the page. Design matters, but confused copy cannot be saved by motion. Step 4 of her process, about writing better copy line by line, is paywalled in this corpus. We teach steps 1 through 3, which are public.

## Step 1: a short messaging guide

Kramer says most startup messaging guides are about nine pages too long. They take ages to write and then nobody uses them. Keep the top-level messaging under one page. Build blocks that can turn into copy for the site and for other channels.

Do some basic audience and competitive work first, so the blocks are not invented in a vacuum. Then write top-level blocks for your primary audience:

- The problem you solve
- The product or solution
- Who it is for
- Why it is better

Get agreement on those blocks before you write website sentences. Messaging is not copy. Messaging is the intent. Copy is the exact wording for a channel.

Then add supporting blocks:

- Benefits, each with proof such as a feature or a figure you can actually stand behind
- Use cases, tied to people
- How the product works, in about three clear steps, especially if the category is new or the product is complex

If you have more than one audience, repeat the top-level blocks for the next audience. Do not try to make one hero sentence please everyone.

## Step 2: a layout people can scan

Kramer warns against pages that reinvent navigation, stack animations, and hide the point. Early teams often do not have a web department. A simple scrolling page is easier to maintain and easier to understand.

Her suggested sequence for an early or growth-stage B2B homepage:

1. Top navigation, with the important links and one primary action
2. Hero, with the top-level blocks and an optional product image, diagram, or video
3. Social proof, usually logos, sometimes quotes, with a heading that names who the customers are
4. Two or three benefit sections, each with a product image
5. Optional use-case or persona section
6. Optional how-it-works section, ideally with a diagram
7. A call-to-action bar just above the footer that restates why someone would act now

The middle order can move if the story needs it. Extra sections such as press logos or security badges can wait.

She prefers product screenshots over generic illustrations in the benefit sections, because the picture itself is a kind of proof. She also says not to get stuck on the words “benefit,” “feature,” and “value prop.” The three benefit blocks should answer why someone needs this and why they should pick you.

On social proof, “companies love us” wastes the heading. Name the group: “GTM teams” or “developers” or whoever you actually serve.

If you have several audiences, the persona section can route people to later pages so the homepage does not try to speak to everyone at once. Those extra pages do not all have to exist on day one.

## Step 3: turn blocks into section copy

Now write. The hero has to cover problem, product, audience, and difference without a pile of extra buttons. Kramer uses Anrok as an example: sales tax compliance is manual; they offer automation for SaaS companies; it ties into the financial stack; it is for SaaS startups, and could be even clearer about finance and operations teams.

The last bar above the footer is smaller than the hero. It restates the reason to act now.

Do not invent statistics to fill a proof slot. If you do not have a figure, use a specific true mechanism (“import the list you already have”) or leave the slot for later.

## A concrete example

A shared shopping-list product:

Hero problem: housemates still fight about who forgot the milk. Product: a list that shows who is grabbing what, live. Audience: people who already share a kitchen and a group chat. Difference: the chat is where the fight happens; this is where the decision is visible.

Social proof heading: not “teams love us,” but “used by people who share a kitchen.” Benefits might be “see it before you double-buy,” “end the ‘I thought you were getting it’ loop,” and “it works when you are standing in the aisle.” Proof can be a screenshot of a checked-off milk line, not a fake percentage.

## Hypothetical Maal example

This example is invented for teaching.

The hero would say that dinner time is a decision problem, that Maal decides dinner, that it is for people who already know ADHD makes 17:40 hard, and that it is not another planner to maintain. A benefits section would not lead with “AI-powered personalization.”

## What to do

**Under 2 minutes.** Look at any homepage, including a draft of yours. Note what it says about problem, product, audience, and difference. Mark missing parts.

**Optional, about 15 minutes.** Sketch the messaging blocks and Kramer’s section list. Write draft lines only for the hero. Do not publish.

## You're done when

You can list the sections, and you have tried to write a hero that covers the four blocks.
