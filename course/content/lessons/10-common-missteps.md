# Eleven missteps worth checking against

About 10 minutes to read. Optional exercise about 10 minutes. These are estimates.

Kunal Gandhi published a thread of marketing mistakes he had been watching in Web3. The ideas travel further than that scene. A few points are crypto-native and will be marked that way.

The root post is the one that announces the thread. Each numbered point is a following post. Both the root and the numbered posts are part of the record.

This is a checklist, not a personality test. If several items feel true, pick one to work on. You do not need to fix eleven things this week.

## 1. Undefined positioning

Kunal says this leads to weak product-market fit, because you were not obsessed enough with users, and to a weak go-to-market plan, because you did not think hard enough about the intended customer, the channels, and how those channels would scale.

If you skipped lessons 5 through 7, this item is pointing you back there.

## 2. Poor strategy

He writes that great strategy beats great execution, and that most teams have a mediocre strategy. Execution still matters. Execution of the right strategy matters more.

This pairs with Brian Balfour’s process-before-tactics warning. A busy calendar is not a strategy.

## 3. Community over product

A great community, he says, is a result of a great product. Not the other way around.

This is easy to ignore if your scene rewards loud chats and member counts. People will not stay in a room that does not help them do the job.

## 4. Skipping a product-marketing approach

He writes the journey as acquisition, then onboarding, then engagement, then retention, then monetization. In his Web3 examples, many projects should have started with smoother onboarding.

The next part of this course is about that journey. You do not win only by getting a click.

## 5. Ignoring founder presence

He says a founder’s presence and participation on crypto Twitter matters, and that personal brands can fuel the project.

The general version: someone visible and trusted has to carry the story. That someone is often the founder at the beginning. It does not mean you must become an influencer, and a later optional lesson covers founder presence with more care. It does mean hiding completely while hoping “the brand” talks is usually a delay.

## 6. Content mistakes

His list: not publishing high-quality work consistently, missing chances to join a narrative people already care about (he calls this thunderclap content), having no distribution plan for the content, and making the work hard to understand.

Consistently does not mean a daily streak. It means the work has a rhythm you can keep, and a way to travel past the people who already follow you.

## 7. Lazy influencer use

Mediocre influencers, he writes, can bring large numbers that do not turn into anything substantial. Prefer people who can carry the right narrative to the right audience.

If you do not use influencers at all, read this as a warning about borrowed audiences in general: a big mention from the wrong room is noise.

## 8. Ignoring search

He calls search the most rewarding content distribution strategy, and says many high-search Web3 phrases were not being served with quality writing.

The general version is the same. People type questions into search boxes and into assistants. Later lessons cover both ordinary search and being cited by AI answers. You do not need to do them this week. You do need to stop treating search as an afterthought.

## 9. Treating launches as announcements

He says teams fail when they do not treat launches as small go-to-market moments, and when they ignore video in those moments. He points at thirdweb as a team that did this well.

A launch is a chance to name the enemy, show proof, and help the right people take a next step. It is not only a date on a calendar.

## 10. A too-narrow real-world use

He writes that a narrow target group often comes from a lack of real-world use cases, and that consumer applications matter even when people say the industry is “in an infrastructure era.”

The general version: if the only people who understand you are other builders of the same tool, you may not have found the job a stranger would hire you for. This is not an instruction to chase “everyone.” It is an instruction to find a use someone outside your group already has.

## 11. Tokens as a worn-out trick

This point is crypto-specific. He writes that a token is the most native marketing move in Web3, and that it has been used so hard it is no longer much of a marketing move.

The portable warning: a built-in incentive (points, credits, a giveaway, a token) can look like distribution and still attract people who only wanted the incentive. When everyone in a category uses the same bait, it stops being a story.

## How to use the list

Read it once. Circle the item that stings. That item is your next lesson’s real homework, even if the next lesson file has a different title.

If none of the items apply because you have not shipped anything, you are not behind. You now know what to watch for when you do.

## Hypothetical Maal example

This example is invented for teaching.

A Maal-shaped risk might be item 3 (building a loud community around meal talk before the product actually decides dinner) or item 4 (chasing signups without asking what “onboarded” means). Item 11 would not apply.

## What to do

**Under 2 minutes.** Circle the one misstep you are most at risk of.

**Optional, about 10 minutes.** Pick three items. Mark each “true of me,” “not true of me,” or “does not apply,” and add one sentence of evidence.

## You're done when

You have checked yourself against the list, and you know which items are general and which stay crypto-specific.
