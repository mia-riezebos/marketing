# Show proof when you launch

About 12 minutes to read. Optional exercise about 15 minutes. These are estimates.

A launch is the moment you ask strangers to care about something you have been living with for months. Kunal Gandhi says teams fail when they treat that moment as a plain announcement, and when they ignore video. Treat it instead as a small go-to-market: story, proof, and a next step.

Matt Epstein writes that going viral is not magic, and that a launch post has to survive a “why do I care” filter in less than a second. He reports that of more than a thousand companies launching on X over a year, 90 percent did fewer than 30,000 views, and that 750 of the weaker pool later shut down. He also reports that his company has done about 200 million views across more than 30 launches and booked more than 2,000 demos. Those numbers are his claims. They explain why he takes packaging seriously. They are not a promise about your launch, and this lesson will not ask you to launch.

## The post has to stand alone

Epstein’s practical rule: do not spread one launch across a handful of posts and hope they add up. On X, he writes, the system keeps one post per conversation in the feed. If you split the story, people who do not already follow you may never see the other pieces.

The first line has to give a stranger a reason to care. He lists openings that usually fail: “we’re excited to announce,” a logo animation, a niche category label, and a founder talking for a long time before anything happens. Things that tend to pass the filter, in his examples, are a number that signals stakes, a fight people already have an opinion about, or a result so large it sounds like it needs checking.

His better habit is to start from the conversation already happening. The six narrative questions from the story lesson belong here again. A claim should attach to something people already feel. Write so a non-expert can care, and an expert can care more.

## Show the extreme true thing

A narrative makes someone stop. A demo makes them believe you.

Epstein’s two questions: what is the most extreme thing your product can do, and how do you make people believe it happened? Then show those moments. He uses a Tesla Roadster stunt as an example of proof that is hard to dismiss as computer graphics, especially when it happens live. You do not need a stunt track. You need the strongest true use, filmed or screenshotted so a skeptic can see the starting state and the result.

This sits next to Andy Raskin’s surviving advice: present evidence. Early customer results beat hypothetical praise. A live demo beats a claim.

For a spreadsheet-to-app tool, Juan’s example is better than a slogan: show the messy sheet, show what the tool produced, and show what you still had to fix. Specificity gives other people something to talk about.

## Followers and connections

Epstein separates two kinds of people. A follower can see your post. A connection is someone who will answer you, and who has an audience of their own. When that person quotes you, their quote is a new post to their audience. That is a different kind of reach than hoping your own followers are enough.

He describes ranking people you already know (investors, early customers, former colleagues) and asking some of them, personally, to quote the launch with a useful angle. He contrasts a weak ask (“would love a retweet”) with a stronger one that includes an example of a good quote.

This course does not ask you to send those messages. Learn the mechanism so you do not confuse “we have followers” with “someone trusted will put this in front of new people.” If you ever do ask, ask people you already know, give them a specific useful angle, and accept no as an answer. Do not turn a launch into unsolicited pitching.

## Give other people something to add

Juan writes about launches that collect “congrats” and then go quiet. The missing piece is often that nobody can see what they would add.

Before you publish, he says, ask what a person who does not know you could add. If you cannot answer, the post is too vague.

The same launch can support several kinds of quotes:

- The founder’s path to the problem
- What the product changes in someone’s actual work
- A recorded reaction that pauses on surprising or doubtful moments
- A specific opinion you are willing to defend with evidence
- An honest tension, such as who is helped and who might be threatened

Disagreement can bring attention. Juan’s caution: think about what that attention will leave people believing, and prefer conversations your team can answer with evidence.

He also describes briefing people who might talk about the launch (what it does, what they can test, what their audience might care about, the evidence, and their own take) and then working the replies as a team afterward. Those are operator methods for a live launch. The paper version, for this course, is to write the angles and the brief you would give, and stop there.

## A concrete example

A shared shopping-list launch that says “introducing HouseholdSync 2.0” is an announcement. A stronger stand-alone moment would show two people about to double-buy milk, then one checkmark appearing while the other is still in the aisle. A quote angle might be “this is what group chats cannot do.” A tension angle might be “shared lists sound small until you have split a deposit over forgotten cleaning supplies.”

## Hypothetical Maal example

This example is invented for teaching.

An announcement would be “Maal is live.” A launch moment would show 17:40, an open fridge, and a decided dinner appearing without a planning session. A quote angle might be “this is a decision-remover, not another meal planner.” Nobody needs to post that for you to write it down.

## What to do

**Under 2 minutes.** Write one sentence a stranger could care about, and one proof, without the word announce.

**Optional, about 15 minutes.** Write the six narrative answers if you do not have them, three quote angles, and one extreme true demo moment. Do not publish and do not ask anyone to share.

## You're done when

You can tell a launch from an announcement, and you have a proof moment written down.
