# Seventy-seven brands, used as inspiration

About 6 minutes to read. Optional exercise about 6 minutes. These are estimates.

Robbie Richards published a long study of how 77 well-known companies found early customers. The opening, which is what we teach from, says the hard shared problem is finding leads and turning them into paying customers, and that there is no plug-and-play formula. Every industry is different.

He then offers the catalog as inspiration (Airbnb, PayPal, Evernote, and many others appear as examples in the full piece). This course will not retell those case studies. Copying a waiting-list stunt or a referral loop because a famous company used it is tactic-first thinking, which Balfour already warned against.

Read a story later if you want color. Then ask the questions you already have: who already gathers, what channel you can keep, and what would count as success. Two fitted ideas beat 77 copied ones.

## A concrete way to use a famous story

Mailbox’s famous waiting list, which Robbie’s catalog highlights, worked because the product was not available yet and curiosity was the scarce resource. If your product is already available and the problem is trust, a waiting list is theater. Evernote’s early content engine is closer to Suraj and Andrei’s page work than to a stunt. PayPal’s referral story depended on a two-sided payment network you probably do not have.

Write the constraint first (no budget, no audience, a local service, a private beta). Then ask which famous move even could survive that constraint. Most will fall away. That is the catalog doing its job.

## What to do

**Under 2 minutes.** Write one famous story you know and why it might not fit.

**Optional, about 6 minutes.** Write two first-customer ideas that fit your constraints.

## You're done when

The catalog is inspiration, not a checklist.
