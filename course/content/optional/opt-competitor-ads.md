# Read competitor ads as research

About 9 minutes to read. Optional exercise about 10 minutes. These are estimates.

Din writes a loud post about starting a LinkedIn outreach agency: read competitor ads, turn them into direct messages with an assistant, send them to a client’s buyers, automate the rest, and make about $20,000 a month. The income line is a pitch. The research half is usable without any of the sending.

Korzhov’s later marketing-engineering list includes a nearby step: rebuild competitor winners. Same instinct. Inspect what already got paid placement.

## What to look at

Din says every LinkedIn ad is a message a company paid to show buyers, so the problem it opens with is a problem your message could open with too. He points at LinkedIn’s ad library and says anyone can see the ads a company ran, without a special account.

His inspection list:

- The problem the ad opens with
- What it offers (a demo, a free audit, a download)
- What proof it shows

He also says to ask which companies a seller already loses deals to, then look at those advertisers for the last month. Only study offers that already sell (he uses a published case-study number as a clue).

You can do a paper version with any ad you have already seen, on any platform. You do not need to open LinkedIn for the exercise, and this lesson will not ask you to log in.

Write down the three parts in the advertiser’s words. Then ask whether you can support a similar claim with your own proof. Copying a number you did not earn is not research. It is a lie.

## The half we will not assign

The rest of Din’s post is an outreach business:

- Find companies hiring sales development roles, because they already pay for booked calls
- Message the founder and offer a test: 1,000 DMs for $500
- Turn ad language into those DMs
- Automate follow-up

Unsolicited volume messaging can break platform rules, privacy expectations, and ordinary courtesy. A $500 test of 1,000 messages is still a thousand people who did not ask. This course will not ask you to send them, to pitch founders, or to spend the $500.

If you later do outbound with permission and clear rules, the research notes are still the right input: start from a problem someone already paid to name, and attach proof you actually have.

## A concrete example

A project-management tool’s ad that opens with “your team updated the deck in three different files again” is naming a fight. The offer might be a shared workspace. The proof might be a logo row. Your notes should capture that fight. They should not invent “used by 10,000 teams” because the ad felt confident.

## What to do

**Under 2 minutes.** From an ad you remember, write problem, offer, and proof.

**Optional, about 10 minutes.** Outline a three-competitor research pass. Draft a message only if you also write why you will not send it.

## You're done when

You can separate library research from outreach, and you have practiced only the research half.
