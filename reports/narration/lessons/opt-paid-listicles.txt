Paid listicles and other sharp link tactics

Many “organic” mentions are paid. Simon describes buying your way into ranking listicles. Some other link tactics, including exchanges, can collide with search-engine rules.

Takeaway: You can understand how paid placements work without pretending they are free, and without running them as homework.

This lesson is optional. It teaches tactics people actually use to appear in “best of” articles and other links. It does not ask you to buy a placement, send email, or exchange links.

“Organic” is often paid

Simon Høiberg writes that many people think organic marketing is free, and that the vast majority of organic mentions they see are paid. In his view it is not very different from ads, only less obvious. He names word of mouth and product-led growth as exception cases.

That is his claim about the current landscape. You do not have to agree that exceptions are rare. You should stop assuming every list that ranks is an unpaid love letter.

Vik and others later amplified the point. Simon authored the method that follows.

How the listicle buy works

People asked him where to buy backlinks, as if there were a clean marketplace. He says marketplaces exist and tells people not to use them. The boring version:

- Search Google or ChatGPT for a query you imagine your buyers typing, where you would like to be recommended.
- Notice that the answers recommend competitors.
- Open the sources. Many will be listicles.
- Find who runs those pages (he mentions using an assistant to sniff emails).
- Email them, skip fake flattery such as “your post really stood out,” say how you found them, and offer to pay to be included.
- Some will refuse. Some will take the money. Repeat.

Costs and conditions: you are paying for a mention, so budget and negotiation are real. The page may be a thin roundup that exists to sell slots. Assistants and search engines that already cite that page may start citing you, or they may not. A placement on a list nobody trusts is a receipt, not a customer.

Risks:

- Advertising and consumer rules in many places expect paid mentions to be disclosed. “As featured in” without saying you paid is the kind of line that gets people into trouble.
- Search engines have long treated paid links that pass ranking credit as a spam problem when they are disguised as editorial votes. Buying a slot and hoping it “counts as organic” is exactly the grey zone.
- Scraping inboxes and sending cold email has its own legal and spam-filter costs (see the cold-email optional lesson). This course will not ask you to send those notes.

If you ever use the tactic, write a true disclosure you can live with, and prefer lists a real buyer would read anyway.

Nearby link tactics, including exchanges

Borja published a long ranked menu of backlink tactics (he says 69 tactics behind 133k AI mentions for his own site). The useful adjacent ideas, in his S-tier language:

- Get into lists buyers already read. Find competitor roundups and the roundups assistants cite. Offer a missing buyer need, a tested feature, and the exact sentence you want added so the edit is easy.
- Publish original statistics people can cite, with sample and method, written as complete sentences, at stable URLs.
- Answer journalist requests where you have real experience.

He also ranks exchanging relevant editorial links, joining networks to find those partners, and logging what was published.

Link exchanges are a known risk under Google’s spam policies about schemes (links you trade mainly to manipulate ranking). A genuine citation in a related article is not the same as an industrial swap club. If you cannot explain the placement as something a reader needed, you are closer to the scheme than to editorial sense.

His AI-mention total is his claim. The menu is a vendor-adjacent promotion as well as a method list. Take the mechanisms, not the scoreboard.

Safer neighboring moves already appeared in the core path: improve pages that almost rank, earn listings where buyers look, and be worth citing because you published something checkable.

A concrete example

You search “best shared grocery list for housemates” and find three roundups that never mention you. Simon’s loop would be to note who runs them and decide whether a paid slot, an earned addition (you send a true differentiator plus a suggested sentence), or ignoring a junk list is the right next step. The exercise stops before anyone is contacted.

What to do

Under 2 minutes. Write one buyer query and the type of page that currently answers it.

Optional, about 10 minutes. Outline the loop on paper and draft an unsent request that includes a disclosure line. Do not send it.

You're done when

You can explain how paid listicles work, what they cost, and why undisclosed paid links and industrial exchanges are risky.
