What marketing is

Marketing is inventing something worth talking about, building it so people benefit, telling the right people, and showing up for a long time. Its job is to turn people into customers.

Takeaway: If you only shout about what you already made, you are living in the smallest part of marketing.

Marketing, in everyday talk, often means ads, posts, and clever slogans. That is one slice of the job. If you start there, you will spend a lot of energy yelling about something people do not yet understand or want.

The four steps

Seth Godin describes marketing as four steps, in order.

First, invent a thing worth making, a story worth telling, a contribution worth talking about. If nothing here is interesting, later steps cannot rescue it.

Second, design and build it in a way people will actually benefit from and care about. A clever story about a painful product is still a painful product.

Third, tell the story to the right people in the right way. This is the step most teams get excited about. Posts, pages, emails, and launches live here.

Fourth, show up. Regularly, consistently, and generously, for years, to organize people and build confidence in the change you are asking them to make. Launch day is not the end of marketing. It is closer to the start of this step.

Notice what is missing: a demand that you post every day. Showing up can mean answering questions, improving the first session, or writing one honest page when you have something to say. Consistency is not the same as a streak.

What the work is for

Aneel, writing for people who come from engineering, puts the purpose in blunt terms. You need words for what you built. You need to put it in front of a specific group. You need to help those people compare it with whatever else they could spend time and money on. You need them to have words they can repeat. And you need to make money if you want the work to continue.

He then tightens it: the fundamental purpose of marketing is to turn people into customers. Other scores exist (impressions, visits, signups, leads). If they never become customers, they are the wrong score to worship.

A better product is not a parade

A public preview of John T. Gourville’s Harvard Business Review essay starts from a famous hope: build a better mousetrap and the world will beat a path to your door. The preview’s point is that marketing new products is not that simple. Companies spend to make better things, and buyers still hesitate.

The rest of that essay is behind a paywall in this corpus. Do not pretend we taught Gourville’s unpaid sections. Keep only this caution: usefulness does not automatically create demand.

A concrete example

Imagine you built a shared shopping list for housemates. Features include real-time sync and barcode scan.

Step one is not “real-time sync.” It is something people already feel, such as the fight about who forgot milk. Step two is whether the list actually prevents that fight on a Tuesday night. Step three is telling the story to people who currently use a notes app and still argue. Step four is being present when the first households get confused, not disappearing after the launch post.

If you only do step three, you are advertising. That can work for a while. It is a thin version of the job.

Hypothetical Maal example

This example is invented for teaching.

A launch post that says “ADHD meal planning, now with AI” jumps to step three. The more useful first sentence might be that dinner time is a decision problem, not a recipe problem. Building the product so a tired person gets a decided dinner is step two. Telling that story to people who already know that 17:40 is hard is step three. Helping them through the first week, without turning dinner into another streak, is step four.

What to do

Under 2 minutes. Write one sentence that names the change you want someone to feel. Do not name a feature.

Optional, about 8 minutes. List Godin’s four steps. Mark each one “I do this,” “I skip this,” or “I do not have this yet.”

You're done when

You can say the four steps without looking, and you know which one you have been treating as the whole job.

Visual explainer

The work gets wider before it gets louder

Use this model to see why “just promote it harder” is usually a step-three answer to a step-one or step-two problem.

Invent something worth making

Find the change, contribution, or tension that is interesting enough to earn attention in the first place.

This is where a product idea or angle becomes worth talking about.

Build it so people benefit

Make the product or service actually relieve the problem instead of relying on story alone.

A strong story cannot save a painful first experience.

Tell the right people in the right way

Pages, posts, launches, and outreach belong here, after the product and angle are coherent.

This is the slice most teams call marketing, but it is only one step.

Show up consistently

Support, iteration, repetition, and long-term presence are part of the job, not optional cleanup after launch.

Consistency is not a streak; it is sustained helpful presence.
