Make the difference obvious

Positioning is the work of putting your real strength at the center, in a market frame that makes that strength easy to see. It is not a fill-in-the-blank slogan.

Takeaway: If people compare you with the wrong alternative, they will miss what you are actually good at.

Positioning is how you help someone place you. It answers “what is this, compared with what, and why would I care?” If that answer is fuzzy, people will pick a comparison for you, and they often pick the wrong one.

April Dunford writes that the most common problem she sees, in startups and large companies, is the same: teams make something excellent, and customers cannot see why it matters. The unique strength feels obvious inside the company. Outside, people ask “so how are you different from Company XYZ?”

Kunal Gandhi later pointed at Dunford’s essay as a lasting bookmark, and he published a shorter six-question worksheet of his own. Use both. Dunford authored the five-step method. Kunal authored the six questions and the warning about undefined positioning.

Why the default category is a trap

Dunford’s early-career example is a team of database experts who built something that could analyze huge amounts of data very fast. When they sold it as a database, many people would not even take the meeting. They already had Oracle. “Database” meant a long list of jobs their product was not trying to do. Their real strength, fast analysis, looked like a side feature.

They later positioned the same product as a business intelligence tool. That frame put fast analysis at the center and escaped the “how are you better than Oracle?” trap.

The lesson is not “always pick a trendier category.” The lesson is: the market name you inherited from your origin story may highlight what you cannot do.

Why a one-line positioning statement is not the work

Dunford argues against the classic fill-in-the-blank positioning statement. She gives three reasons.

It assumes the right position is already sitting in your head, waiting to be written down. In fact a product can be positioned in several ways, and the choice changes the business.

The output is an awkward sentence that teams cannot remember or use. It gets written once and never opened again.

Finishing the sentence creates a false feeling of being done. Markets move. Strengths that were unique become table stakes. Positioning has to be reviewed, which is why she dates the written version.

Five steps

Dunford’s steps are easy to name and not easy to do.

1. Let go of where you came from. Write down the original idea of what you set out to build, then set it aside long enough to see what you actually deliver today.

2. Isolate your uniqueness. List what you have that others do not: a feature, a process, a relationship, a business model. Do not judge yet whether each item is “good.” Even complexity can be valuable to the right buyer. Dunford’s IBM example: heavy customization made the product harder for beginners and was exactly why some customers chose it.

3. Translate uniqueness into value. Customers do not buy your patented query method. They buy “we can answer the question while the customer is still on the phone.” Stay open about who might want that value.

4. Ask who cares a lot. Many people care a little. A few people care in a way that makes the sale faster and discounts rarer. “Companies with a lot of data” was too wide in her database story. Companies that needed to analyze huge amounts of machine-generated data for security or customer service cared a lot. If you had to sell as much as possible this month, who would you call first, and how would you find them?

5. Choose the market frame that makes the strength obvious. When you say “this is a CRM” or “this is a database,” you inherit assumptions about competitors, features, and price. There is almost always more than one possible frame. You can try to win an existing market, own a segment, change how people think about a category, or (hardest) name a new one.

Then write it down, date it, and test it. Positioning is not messaging. Messaging is the later, channel-specific language that sits on top of this work.

A change in positioning is a change in the business. Sales comparisons, pricing expectations, the roadmap, and support all shift. If only the homepage sentence changes, you have not really repositioned.

Kunal’s six questions

If Dunford’s essay feels long, start with Kunal’s shorter list. Open a document and answer, without wandering:

- What do we do? What problem do we solve?
- In which market category do we compete?
- How is our approach the best? What sets us apart?
- What unique attributes or features do we have, and what key benefits follow?
- Who would be most interested? How would we recognize them?
- If not us, what would they choose instead?

His later warning is that undefined positioning leads to weak product-market fit (not being obsessed enough with users) and a weak go-to-market plan (not thinking hard enough about the intended customer, the channels, and how those channels would scale).

The two worksheets fit together. His questions 3 and 4 are Dunford’s uniqueness and value. His question 5 is “who cares a lot.” His question 2 and Dunford’s step 5 are the market frame. His question 6 forces you to name the real alternative, which might be “they keep using a spreadsheet,” not a branded competitor.

A concrete example

A shared shopping-list app could sit in “household productivity tools,” where it is a weaker notes app. It could sit in “ways to stop the weekly fight about who forgot milk,” where real-time sync and a “someone already grabbed it” checkmark become the center. The alternative is not another list app. The alternative is a group chat that already exists and already causes the fight.

Hypothetical Maal example

This example is invented for teaching.

The default category is “AI meal planner.” That frame invites comparison with every recipe app and macro tracker. A sharper frame might be “dinner as a decision-remover,” for people who already know that 17:40 is the hard part. The alternative is not another planner. The alternative is standing in front of the fridge and giving up.

What to do

Under 2 minutes. Write your default category and one other category that would make your strength easier to see.

Optional, about 15 minutes. Complete Dunford’s five steps in rough notes, or complete Kunal’s six questions. Date the page.

You're done when

You can explain the default-category trap, and you have written who might care a lot about the value you uniquely offer.

Name the real strength

Start with the thing you genuinely do better or more cleanly than alternatives.

Positioning begins with reality, not with a slogan workshop.

Notice the wrong comparison

If buyers compare you to the wrong alternative, your real edge disappears before it can matter.

This is why strong products still sound ordinary in the wrong category.

Choose the frame that makes the strength legible

Shift the story so people evaluate you against the right baseline, problem, or substitute.

The goal is not novelty for its own sake; it is easier understanding.
