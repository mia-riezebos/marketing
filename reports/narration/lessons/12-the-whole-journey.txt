Think in the whole journey

Product marketing is a stack of questions covering how people find you, how they start, why they stay, and how you get paid. Acquisition is only the first layer.

Takeaway: If you only measure new visitors, you will not notice that people arrive, get confused, and leave.

A launch post can bring people to the door. Product marketing asks what happens after they walk in.

Kunal Gandhi says crypto teams should take a product-marketing approach from the start. The portable version does not require a crypto product. It is a stack of questions whose answers become product work, not only ads.

He writes the stack as acquisition, onboarding, engagement and retention, and monetization. The same sequence appeared in his earlier list of missteps: teams skip this approach, and many should have started with smoother onboarding.

In a first-week plan he adds that you should understand the full journey with the people who build the product. Marketing is not a separate department that receives a finished object.

Acquisition

This layer is how the right people find you. Kunal’s notes:

- Define the intended user. Writing a full profile is even better.
- Pick channels.
- Map content loops and campaigns.
- Line up partnerships or integrations that already sit near those users.
- Consider a referral program only if a happy user would naturally tell someone.

Notice what is missing: “be everywhere.” Channel choice still follows the one-channel lesson.

Onboarding

Onboarding is the path from “I arrived” to “I get it.”

Decide what “onboarded” means in observable terms. In Kunal’s crypto examples that might be a connected wallet or a first transaction. In a more ordinary product it might be “invited one housemate” or “imported an existing list” or “got a decided dinner on the first night.”

Then design the first moment the product proves itself. If that moment is buried behind a tour, a survey, and a settings page, people will leave before they understand you.

This is why homepage copy and onboarding are related. The promise you made on the first screen has to come true quickly inside the product.

Engagement and retention

Kunal writes that acquisition is easy and retention is hard. In another post he says product shelf life is now short, and that most of a marketer’s time should go into how people stay.

Questions that belong here:

- Which part of the product would make someone come back without a reminder?
- How does a casual user become someone who relies on it?
- Why do people leave, and what would a honest win-back look like?
- What can you measure without building a warehouse of dashboards on day one?

His examples include points, drops, and on-chain metrics because he is writing to crypto teams. Translate the questions, not the props. A household list app might care about “second shop completed this week,” not about points.

Monetization

Write down why you would charge, how, and for what. Kunal notes that crypto culture often loves free products, and that sustainable revenue still has to be thought about early.

You do not need a clever pricing page this week. You do need to stop pretending the question can wait forever. Free can be a real strategy. Accidental free, with no idea how the work continues, is just a delay.

A concrete example

For the shared shopping list:

Acquisition might be search for “shared grocery list” plus a card that movers or housemates already hand each other.

Onboarding might mean “two people checked the same list.” The first proof is seeing someone else grab milk before you leave the house.

Retention might mean the list is still used on the third shop, not that you sent a streak notification. Monetization might be a family plan, or it might honestly stay free until you know what people would pay for.

Hypothetical Maal example

This example is invented for teaching.

Acquisition: people who already describe dinner-time shutdown as the problem. Onboarding: a decided dinner on night one, without building a pantry profile first. Retention: they came back the next hard evening without being nagged by a streak. Monetization: whatever model does not turn dinner into another chore to maintain.

What to do

Under 2 minutes. Write what “started successfully” means, as something a person did.

Optional, about 10 minutes. Fill four boxes (acquisition, onboarding, retention, monetization) with questions, plus one note on what you will not measure yet.

You're done when

You can name the layers, and you have defined a successful first use in plain words.
