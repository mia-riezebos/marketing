Improve what already almost ranks

The fastest search win is often a page search already notices. Add the missing answers, put the main question first, and test titles that use the words people type.

Takeaway: A new article is not always the next move. A page on page two, missing the phrases people already search, is often the better patient.

Andrei Saioc’s point is blunt. Do not start by writing new articles or buying links. Start with the pages search already likes, and with the questions those pages almost answer.

He uses Google Search Console, often shortened to GSC: a free report that shows which of your pages appeared for which searches, how often, how many clicks they received, and roughly where they sat. If you do not have it set up, you can still learn the loop on paper. This lesson will not ask you to connect an account.

Borja later focuses on one high-leverage piece of that loop: the title. A page can already sit on page one and still lose the click because the title does not sound like the search.

Andrei’s loop

Look at pages first, sorted by clicks and then by impressions. Take a small set, such as the top ten. Those pages already have some trust, so a fix there often pays faster than a brand-new URL.

For each page, look at the queries (the searches that showed it):

- Queries that get impressions even though the article never mentions them
- Queries sitting around positions 10 to 30 (roughly page two and three)

The second group is his “goldmine.” Search already thinks you are relevant. The page is just incomplete.

He uses an assistant as a writing assistant, not as the writer. It should find the gaps. You should fill them. His prompt pattern asks the assistant to say where each missing query belongs: a new heading, a new paragraph, a short FAQ item, or a tweak to a sentence. Then rewrite only those parts.

Two writing rules sit on top of the data:

Answer the main question in the first two paragraphs. No “in today’s digital world,” and no long story before the point. Readers decide in seconds. Search does too.

Put the main phrase in the title, the description, and the first paragraph. He says this sounds basic, and that many already-ranking pages miss at least one.

Then make the page yours. Add screenshots of you doing the thing, simple tables or diagrams, your own data or opinions, and an angle the current top results do not cover. He insists those few sentences be written by you. An assistant cannot screenshot your actual screen.

Link the improved pages to each other where a reader would actually click, and point newer pages at them. After you change a live page, requesting a fresh index and waiting one or two weeks is how you see whether the queries moved. Then repeat.

Only after that does he talk about links: a few a month, including free ones such as relevant directories, guest conversations, partner pages, or answering journalist requests, pointed at the pages that already almost rank.

He says give the loop one to three months. That is his timing claim, not a guarantee.

Titles that sound like the search

Borja says that if you are tired of waiting months, the fastest test is often the title of a page that already ranks. He notes public court and leak discussion that clicks influence ranking, and that a new title on an already-known page can show up in Search Console in about two weeks. Those are his reasons for treating titles as a fast test, not a promise that Google will reward every rewrite.

How to pick a patient: pages in roughly positions 3 to 10, with at least a couple of hundred impressions, and a click-through rate well under what that position usually gets. He mentions rough benchmarks (about 9 percent at position 3, 6 percent at 4, 4 percent at 5) and a rule of thumb that a top-ten page getting around 40 percent fewer clicks than its benchmark is a title suspect.

Then search the query in a private window. See the title Google actually shows (it sometimes rewrites yours) and what the results above you promise. If Google rewrites you, it often grabs your main heading, so your heading and title should say the same thing.

On about 90 days of his own click data, titles that included the words people typed (such as “QuickBooks alternatives”) got a 4.7 percent click-through rate. Titles that only named the category (such as “best accounting software”) got under 1 percent. That is his reported split, not a law of the internet. The useful idea is still clear: sound like the search, not only like your category.

Write at least three titles. Put the searcher’s words first. Make a specific promise. Give a reason to open the page now. Stay under about 60 characters so the line is less likely to be cut off. Do not promise what the page does not deliver.

Change one thing at a time, and write down the date, clicks, and click-through rate first. Compare similar positions. If the page jumps from position 6 to position 3, the click-through rate will move even if the title did nothing. Judge the test by clicks as well as rate, because a better title can attract extra long-tail searches: impressions rise, the rate can dip, and clicks can still be up.

Borja also mentions testing top title ideas as small search ads (he names about 20 dollars a day, and keeping a winner after about 1,000 impressions each). That is a paid shortcut. This course will not ask you to spend it. You can learn the same writing rules on paper.

His wider SEO menu, including a claim of 128,000 AI mentions, is a large ranked list and a vendor promotion. The title method above is the part that belongs in this lesson.

A concrete example

A page titled “Our services” ranks somewhere on page two for “same-week chair repair.” The article never mentions “landlord inspection” or “deposit,” even though those queries already show impressions. Andrei’s move is to answer those questions on the same page and to put the answer near the top. Borja’s move is to try titles such as “Same-week chair repair before a landlord inspection” instead of “Quality furniture services.”

Hypothetical Maal example

This example is invented for teaching.

If a page about deciding dinner appeared for “ADHD what to cook tonight” but never used that phrase, the first two paragraphs should answer it. A category title like “Smart meal planning” would be the weak version. A searcher-word title would mention ADHD and dinner-time decisions, and the page would have to keep that promise.

What to do

Under 2 minutes. Rewrite one title with the searcher’s words first.

Optional, about 15 minutes. Write the main answer in two short paragraphs, draft three titles, and note one screenshot or table worth adding. Do not buy ads and do not publish if you do not want to.

You're done when

You can explain the loop and the title test, and you have written titles without spending money.
