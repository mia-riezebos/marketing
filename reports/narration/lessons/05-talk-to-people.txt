Talk to people who already have the problem

Interviews beat guesses. Ask about the last time the problem happened, what they already tried, and what they wish existed. Do not pitch while you listen.

Takeaway: Your customer already has words for the problem. Borrow those words before you invent slogans.

If you invent the customer’s sentences, your page will sound like a brochure. If you collect the sentences they already use when they are tired, stuck, or embarrassed, your page can sound like them.

An interview here is a conversation with a purpose. It is not a pitch meeting and it is not a survey with twenty boxes.

What you are listening for

Guillaume Bardet describes a fast way to gather raw material (reviews, competitor pages, forums) and pressure-test a go-to-market idea with an assistant. He ends on the part that matters for this lesson: that work is still a hypothesis. The real work starts when you talk to actual buyers. “Customer conversations are the only thing that matters,” he writes. Roman later shared that post. Guillaume authored it.

Kunal Gandhi says the same thing in plainer operator language. Get fanatical about who your users are. Do everything you can to understand who you are marketing to. Have a conversation with people you believe are the customer base. In a first-week plan he also writes: talk to people, not at them, and make early users feel seen.

Ask what happened last time, not what they might do in theory. Theory answers are polite. Incident answers are useful.

Listen for four things.

The trigger: what started the last messy incident.

The workaround: what they already tried, including messy things like spreadsheets, group chats, or giving up.

The cost: time, money, shame, or a fight with someone they live with.

The wish: the sentence that starts with “I just want…”

Those wishes are often better headlines than anything you would invent.

How to ask without pitching

Start with permission from someone you already know, or write the interview as a letter to your past self. This course does not ask you to message strangers.

Useful questions sound like this:

- “Walk me through the last time this went badly.”
- “What did you try that day?”
- “What have you already paid for, or given up on?”
- “If something worked, what would it take off your plate?”

Stay quiet after they answer. The extra sentence people add when you do not fill the silence is often the good one.

Do not say “would you use my product?” That question trains them to be nice. Do not demo in the middle. Demo later, after you have their words on paper.

How this sits next to desk research

Reviews and forum threads are useful (the next lesson shows how to gather them). They are still other people’s writing, filtered by a platform. A conversation lets you ask “what happened last Thursday?” and hear the pause before the answer.

This course does not ask you to message strangers, buy an email list, or pitch in someone else’s community. Practice with a person who already trusts you, or write the interview as a letter to your past self about the last real incident.

April Dunford’s later positioning work depends on this kind of listening. “Who cares a lot” is not a demographic you invent. It is a pattern you notice in people who already feel the value.

A concrete example

You are exploring a shared-calendar tool for separated parents. A polite survey answer is “we need better communication.” An incident answer might be: “Last Thursday the school play was in her calendar and not mine, and our kid thought I forgot.”

The headline hiding in that sentence is not “shared calendars with sync.” It is “I don’t want my kid to think I forgot.”

Hypothetical Maal example

This example is invented for teaching.

Someone might say they do not need another meal planner. They might say they stand in the kitchen at 17:40, open the fridge, and lose twenty minutes deciding. “I just want dinner to be decided” is their line. “AI-powered personalization” is yours. Use theirs.

What to do

Under 2 minutes. Write three questions about the last time the problem happened. None of them may mention your product.

Optional, about 12 minutes. Interview one person you already know, or interview yourself about the last real incident. Write five phrases they used, copied as closely as you can.

You're done when

You have three product-free questions and a few words that did not come from your own draft.
