Write one idea people can finish

A useful post has one idea, a takeaway, and a first line that earns the second. Clarity beats cleverness. Conversations you already had can become more than one piece of writing.

Takeaway: If a tired person cannot tell what they are supposed to walk away with, the post is not finished.

Ryan’s copywriting guide is about turning an idea into words someone will actually finish. He reports very large impression totals and says he added about 9,000 followers and about 6 million impressions in his first month of posting. Those are his claims about his own account. They are not a quota for you, and this lesson will not ask you to post.

Grant Miller later shared the guide and highlighted that early growth. Ryan authored the method.

Four fundamentals

Good copywriting, he writes, gets the right person to take the right action. That action might be reading, replying, sharing, following, or buying. The post should be built for one of those.

Four principles sit under everything else:

- Write for a specific audience. Picture the reader. What are they stuck on, where do they already read, what do they want, what do they feel right now? If they are not on the platform you chose, the post is already aimed at the wrong room.
- One idea per post. If you have three ideas, you have three posts. Extra ideas confuse the reader and, he argues, the ranking system too.
- Every post needs a takeaway. What does someone walk away with? If you cannot answer, keep writing privately.
- Clarity beats cleverness. Reduce friction. His personal rule is that if a twelve-year-old cannot read the sentences, he will not publish. The idea can still be grown-up. The words and the layout should not show off.

The first line earns the second

A hook is the opening. Its only job is to get someone to read the next line. You are trying to create a gap they want closed, and to make them feel spoken to.

Ryan lists eight hook types worth practicing:

- A bold claim
- A callout to a specific kind of reader
- A curiosity gap
- A clear outcome
- A contrarian line
- A story opening
- A numbered list
- Proof

The strongest openings often combine types, and they tap self-interest, curiosity, novelty, emotion, or identity. “If you share a kitchen with someone (identity), this is how we stopped buying milk twice (self-interest plus a gap)” is more useful than “excited to share an update.”

His process: write the post first, the hook last. Draft five to ten openings. Cut the weak ones. Refine what remains. Lead with the most interesting thing. Be specific: names, numbers you can stand behind, and time frames beat vague growth claims. Keep it short. If the best line is at the bottom, move it up.

Do not invent proof. If you have no result yet, use a specific true observation instead of a fake figure.

Shape, rhythm, and the last line

A post needs a shape or it reads like a ramble. Structures he uses often include problem then insight then takeaway; a list; a graphic; a video; a longer article; and a quote with added context.

People scan. Use line breaks. Vary sentence length. Keep each line to one thought. Keep paragraphs to one to three lines, because a four-line block looks huge on a phone. Numbers and lists help scanning. Short connector lines such as “Here is the part most pages skip” pull a reader down.

Word choice: cut filler, prefer simple words, use verbs that do work, write in the active voice, and say “you” more than “I.” Delete any word that does not change the meaning. He also warns against a generic machine-written tone.

Voice is harder to copy than a format. Write the way you talk. Have a point of view. Keep the quirks that are actually yours.

The last line is the second most important line. It should tell the reader what to think or do next: a takeaway in one or two lines, a question, or a pointer to what comes next. Do not fade out.

None of this requires a daily posting quota. Nicholas Dulait’s later note about posting often to grow an account is an optional extra, and even there this course will not turn frequency into a streak.

Conversations as raw writing

Kunal Gandhi describes a tactic he calls conversations as content marketing. A lot of brand writing, he says, sounds like preaching, so it disappears. Instead, have a real conversation with someone in or around your world, then reuse what came out of it: a short video, a thread, a longer post, and smaller posts from one central idea. A conversation with someone who already has a trusted audience can help even more.

Practice this with conversations you already had permission to learn from. Do not record people without consent. Do not message strangers to create homework. A conversation with yourself about the last time the problem happened is enough to try the reuse pattern.

A concrete example

Weak: “We are building the future of household coordination with seamless sync.”

Stronger shape: name the fight about milk, say what most list apps miss (they do not show who is already walking to the fridge), and leave a takeaway (the chat is where the fight happens; the list should prevent the fight). Opening options: a callout to people who share a kitchen, or a tiny story about buying milk twice in one evening.

Hypothetical Maal example

This example is invented for teaching.

One idea: dinner time is a decision problem. Takeaway: a useful product decides dinner instead of assigning a planning project. Hook options: a callout to people who freeze at 17:40, or a contrarian line that another meal planner will not help. Do not post it for the exercise.

What to do

Under 2 minutes. Split a draft so each sentence holds one thought, and put the most interesting line first.

Optional, about 15 minutes. Write one unpublished practice post with one idea, a takeaway, and two different openings. If you want, note how one past conversation could become three shapes.

You're done when

You can name the four fundamentals, and you have a post a tired reader could scan.
